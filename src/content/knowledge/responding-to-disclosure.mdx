## Why this conversation matters

Telling a manager about a diagnosis is a risk from the employee’s point of view. They do not know how you will react, who you will tell, or whether it will change how you see them. Most people who disclose have thought about it for a long time. Your response in the first two minutes will shape whether they trust you, whether they ask for what they need, and whether they stay.

## In the moment

**Thank them.** “Thank you for telling me. I appreciate that.” Say it and mean it.

**Do not react to the label.** You do not need to say what you know about the condition, mention someone else who has it, or reassure them that you would never have guessed. None of that helps.

**Ask what would help.** “Is there anything that would make work easier for you, or anything you’d like me to do differently?” They may have a clear answer, or they may not know yet. Both are fine.

**Ask about confidentiality.** “Who would you like to know about this?” Do not assume they want HR told, colleagues told or the team informed. Some will, most will not.

**Agree a next step.** Even if it is just “let’s talk again in two weeks”.

## Afterwards

- Write down what was agreed, and share it with the employee.
- Keep the information confidential. Health information is special category data under UK data protection law. Share it only with the people the employee has agreed to, and only as much as they need.
- Do the things you said you would do.
- Check back. Adjustments that made sense on day one may need changing.

## Adjustments and the law

Many neurodivergent conditions meet the definition of disability in the Equality Act 2010. Once an employer knows, or could reasonably be expected to know, that an employee has a disability, there is a duty to make reasonable adjustments to remove or reduce disadvantage at work. That duty does not depend on a formal diagnosis being on file.

Most adjustments cost little or nothing: written instructions, a quieter workspace, flexible start times, noise-cancelling headphones, extra time on written tasks, agendas in advance. Where equipment, software or specialist support is needed, the government’s Access to Work scheme can help fund it.

If you are unsure what is reasonable, or the request is larger than you can approve, involve HR. Occupational health can advise on what would help. Legal advice is for the unusual case, not the everyday one.

## What should you never do?

- Ask for proof or medical evidence before taking the conversation seriously.
- Tell anyone without the employee’s agreement.
- Treat the disclosure as an explanation for past performance issues, or as a reason to expect less in future.
- Make the employee the team’s expert on their condition.
- Forget about it.

## What can managers do?

Respond warmly, ask what helps, protect confidentiality, act, and follow up. If you have been doing the everyday things well, asking people how they work best, giving clear written instructions, offering some control over the environment, the disclosure conversation is much easier, because most of what the person needs is already in place.
