## What is it?

Autism is a lifelong difference in how people experience the world, communicate and relate to others. It is not an illness and it is not something to be cured. Autistic people vary enormously; the word “spectrum” describes that variety, not a line from mild to severe.

The NHS estimates that more than one in 100 people are autistic. Many adults are undiagnosed, and many who are diagnosed choose not to tell their employer. The Buckland Review, published by the Department for Work and Pensions in 2024, found that only around three in ten autistic adults are in work, a lower rate than for any other disability group, and that most of the barriers are about how workplaces are run rather than what autistic people can do.

## What might it mean at work?

Differences most often show up in communication and in the environment. An autistic colleague may take instructions literally, prefer precise written information, find small talk or ambiguous social situations draining, and be more sensitive to noise, light and interruption. They may also be exceptionally reliable, thorough and honest, and notice things others miss.

Problems at work are usually mismatches: unclear expectations, unwritten rules, sudden changes, noisy open-plan environments, and feedback that is hinted at rather than stated.

## Possible strengths

- Attention to detail and accuracy
- Deep expertise and sustained focus in areas of interest
- Logical, systematic thinking and pattern recognition
- Honesty, directness and consistency
- Reliability with routines and processes once they are clear
- Fresh perspectives that challenge assumptions

## Possible difficulties

- Ambiguous instructions and unwritten expectations
- Sudden changes to plans, roles or routines
- Sensory environments: noise, lighting, open-plan offices, hot-desking
- Reading social cues, office politics and indirect feedback
- Unstructured social situations, including interviews
- Managing anxiety when things are uncertain

## What can help?

- **Say what you mean.** Clear, specific, written instructions and expectations.
- **Give notice of change** wherever you can, and explain why.
- **Structure meetings** with agendas, purposes and written actions.
- **Offer control over the environment**: a quieter desk, headphones, remote days, a consistent workspace.
- **Give feedback directly and specifically**, without relying on hints.
- **Adapt recruitment**: share interview questions in advance, use work-sample tasks, and judge the job, not the small talk.

## What should you never assume?

- That an autistic person lacks empathy, ambition or social interest.
- That someone is autistic because they are quiet, precise or direct, or is not because they are sociable.
- That what helped one autistic colleague will help another.
- That an autistic colleague wants their diagnosis known, or wants to be the office expert on autism.

## What can managers do?

Ask people how they work best, and act on the answer. Put important things in writing. Give warning of change. Make the environment easier to control. Give direct, specific feedback. These are good management for everyone and they remove most of the barriers autistic colleagues describe.

If an employee tells you they are autistic, thank them, keep it confidential and ask what would help. Autism is generally a disability under the Equality Act 2010, so there may be a legal duty to make reasonable adjustments. Your job is not to diagnose or to become an expert. It is to see the need and respond to it.
